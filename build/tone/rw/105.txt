@p3-50
We follow SAFe, the Scaled Agile Framework, because our engineering organisation is about 100 people across several teams, and the ceremonies work at two levels, basically.

PI planning is the big-picture event. A program increment is several sprints, usually around ten weeks. All the teams plan it together. We look at the features coming up, agree each team's objectives, and map dependencies between teams, like one team needing an API from another before it can finish its feature. Risks are raised openly and someone owns each one. What comes out is a plan everyone has agreed, not one handed down.

Grooming, or backlog refinement, happens during the sprints. We take upcoming stories, clarify what they really mean with the product owner and BA, write clear acceptance criteria, split stories that are too big, and estimate them. In a GxP product, we also agree early what validation evidence each story needs.

Then each sprint has the usual ceremonies. Sprint planning, where we commit to stories based on our capacity. A daily stand-up to share progress and blockers. A sprint review or demo, where we show working software to the product owner and stakeholders and get feedback. And a retrospective, where we talk about what to improve in how we work.

I take part in all of these. Because I also coordinate release integration across [about 5] teams, PI planning is really useful for me, since that's where I spot cross-team dependencies that could affect a release.

@p3-51
I estimate in story points, relative to similar work we've already done, because relative estimates are more reliable than guessing hours, in my experience.

I make sure I understand the story and its acceptance criteria before anything. Then I break it into tasks, which shows the real size and often reveals hidden work. The key is to estimate everything that "done" really needs, not just the coding, unit and integration tests, code review, database scripts, documentation, validation evidence, and release work. In a GxP product, testing and evidence can be as big as the coding itself, and honestly, leaving them out is the most common reason estimates go wrong.

Then I compare with similar stories from the past. Is this bigger or smaller than that one? The team estimates together, say with planning poker, and big differences are useful, because they usually mean someone knows something the others don't.

If there's real uncertainty, like a new library or an unclear integration, I say so openly and suggest a short time-boxed spike to investigate, instead of pretending to know. Like, before adding the Rebex SFTP provider, a short spike against the vendor's test server made the actual estimate much more accurate.

And then we check the total sprint commitment against our past velocity, so we commit to what we can actually finish, rather than what we hope to finish.
