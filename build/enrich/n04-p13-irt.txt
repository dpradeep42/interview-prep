T: What is a clinical trial (clinical study)?
K: clinical trial, clinical study, what is a study, trial phases, protocol, sponsor, cro
X: A clinical trial, which we also call a clinical study, is a research study in people that tests whether a new medicine or treatment is safe and works. Before a medicine can be approved by regulators, like the FDA in the US or the EMA in Europe, it has to go through these trials.
X: There are a few key players. The sponsor is usually the pharmaceutical company that owns the drug and pays for the trial. A CRO, a contract research organisation like IQVIA, runs parts of the trial and provides technology like IRT. Sites are the hospitals and clinics where patients, called subjects, take part, run by investigators and site staff.
X: Every trial follows a protocol, a detailed plan that defines who can join, what treatments are compared, how patients are assigned to them, what happens at each visit, and what is measured. Trials usually run in phases. Phase one tests safety in a small group, phase two tests whether it works and the right dose, and phase three compares it with standard treatment in thousands of patients across many countries.
X: To keep results fair, most trials are randomised, meaning a computer decides by chance which treatment each patient gets, and blinded, meaning patients and doctors don't know which one it is.
X: In IRT, a study is that one trial set up in our system: its protocol rules, countries and sites, treatment arms, visit schedule, drug kits and users. Our software makes sure the randomisation and the drug supply follow the protocol exactly.

T: How does randomisation work in IRT end to end?
V: randFlow
K: randomisation flow, randomize subject, allocation, kit assignment, randomization end to end, transaction engine
X: Randomisation assigns each eligible subject to a treatment arm by chance, following the protocol. In our IRT, a study is one clinical trial set up in the system, with its arms, strata and pre-generated randomisation lists.
X: The flow goes like this. The site user, signed in through Keycloak single sign-on, opens the subject in the legacy MVC portal or the newer React screens and confirms randomisation. T: What are randomisation lists, block randomisation and stratification?
K: randomisation list, block randomization, stratification, strata, allocation ratio, dynamic randomisation, minimisation, pocock simon, permuted block
X: A randomisation list is a pre-generated sequence of treatment assignments, created by a statistician before a clinical trial starts. In our IRT, a study is one trial set up in the system, and its lists are uploaded through our randomisation list management feature, then used slot by slot.
X: Block randomisation, or permuted blocks, keeps the arms balanced as enrolment goes on. For a two-arm study with a one-to-one ratio and a block size of four, each block contains two of each arm, in a random order. So after every four subjects, the arms are perfectly balanced, and the block size is kept hidden from sites so nobody can guess the next assignment.
X: Stratification makes sure balance also holds within important groups, called strata, like age band, region or disease severity. Each combination of stratification factors gets its own list, or its own blocks, and a subject takes the next slot from their stratum's list. Lists can be central, or blocked by site or stratum.
X: Dynamic randomisation, or minimisation, is the alternative when there are too many strata for lists. Our system implements the Pocock-Simon method: for each new subject, it calculates which arm would minimise imbalance across all factors, and assigns it with a high probability, keeping some randomness.
X: On top of that, cohort caps limit how many subjects can enrol per arm or cohort. In our code, permuted block and Pocock-Simon are separate implementations behind one randomisation interface, chosen by the study's configuration.

T: What is re-randomisation in IRT?
K: re-randomisation, rerandomisation, rerandomization, re-randomization, rerandomize, second randomization, crossover, extension study, treatment switch
X: Re-randomisation means assigning a subject who is already randomised to a new treatment arm, at a later point in the study, because the protocol says so. In our IRT, a study is one clinical trial set up in the system, and re-randomisation is configured per study.
X: Common reasons are crossover designs, where subjects switch to another treatment after a period, a second treatment stage, where responders and non-responders are randomised again, or moving into an extension phase.
X: Technically, it's a follow-up transaction in our transaction engine. It releases the subject's old assignment, according to the study configuration, and draws a new slot or arm, from a separate list or using the study's crossover configuration, which defines which arms a subject can move to. The subject keeps the full history of both assignments, and every re-randomisation is audited with the reason, who did it and when.
X: The same safeguards as the first randomisation apply. The subject must be in the right state for that stage. A subject can only be re-randomised once per stage, enforced in the database, not just the UI. Blinding is protected, so blinded users never see either arm. And kit dispensing after re-randomisation must match the new arm.
X: [I worked on making the re-randomisation request idempotent, so a double click or a network retry can't re-randomise a subject twice.]

T: What is DARM (Drug Accountability and Return Management)?
V: darmFlow
K: darm, drug accountability, return management, reconciliation, drug destruction, kit returns, return shipment
X: DARM, drug accountability and return management, covers what happens to study drug after it reaches a site, until it's reconciled and destroyed. In our IRT, a study is one clinical trial set up in the system, and in a regulated trial every single kit must be accounted for.
X: The journey goes like this. The site pharmacist records accountability entries per kit: units used, unused, returned or damaged. X: Kit statuses follow that lifecycle: available, in transit, received at site, dispensed, partially used, returned, in transit to depot, received at depot, reconciled and destroyed, plus damaged, lost, quarantined and blocked.
X: Technically, DARM was built in the legacy ASP.NET MVC portal, and it's the module we're migrating to React with TypeScript right now, starting with accountability entry and return shipments, on top of new .NET APIs. There's also a mobile path through our MuleSoft layer.
X: Every step is a validated state change with an audit record, and blinding is protected, so blinded users never see which arm a kit belongs to.

T: What is blinding, and how does emergency unblinding work in IRT?
K: blinding, unblinding, emergency unblinding, double blind, unblinded users, code break, blinded roles
X: Blinding means the people in a clinical trial don't know which treatment a subject is receiving, so expectations can't bias the results. In a double-blind study, neither the subject nor the site staff know. In our IRT, a study is one trial set up in the system, and blinding is enforced at the role and field level.
X: Blinded users never see the arm or kit contents. They see only kit numbers, and kits for different arms look identical. Only specific unblinded roles, like an unblinded pharmacist or a supply manager, can see treatment information, and the APIs and screens are filtered by those roles, not just hidden in the UI. Reports and notifications respect the same rules.
X: Emergency unblinding is for a medical emergency, when a doctor must know the treatment to treat the patient. In our system, it's a privileged transaction. Only authorised roles can perform it, a reason is mandatory, and depending on the study's configuration, the user must re-authenticate with an electronic signature. The system records who unblinded, when and why, in an unblinding history table, can notify the sponsor, and can exclude the subject from further dosing.
X: The engineering principles are least privilege, enforcement in the backend, and a complete, tamper-proof audit trail, because unblinding is one of the first things an inspector checks.
