T: What is a clinical trial (clinical study)?
K: clinical trial, clinical study, what is a study, trial phases, protocol, sponsor, cro
X: A clinical trial, which we also call a clinical study, is a research study in people that tests whether a new medicine or treatment is safe and works. Before a medicine can be approved by regulators, like the FDA in the US or the EMA in Europe, it has to go through these trials.
X: There are a few key players. The sponsor is usually the pharmaceutical company that owns the drug and pays for the trial. A CRO, a contract research organisation like IQVIA, runs parts of the trial and provides technology like IRT. Sites are the hospitals and clinics where patients, called subjects, take part, run by investigators and site staff.
X: Every trial follows a protocol, a detailed plan that defines who can join, what treatments are compared, how patients are assigned to them, what happens at each visit, and what is measured. Trials usually run in phases. Phase one tests safety in a small group, phase two tests whether it works and the right dose, and phase three compares it with standard treatment in thousands of patients across many countries.
X: To keep results fair, most trials are randomised, meaning a computer decides by chance which treatment each patient gets, and blinded, meaning patients and doctors don't know which one it is.
X: In IRT, a study is that one trial set up in our system: its protocol rules, countries and sites, treatment arms, visit schedule, drug kits and users. Our software makes sure the randomisation and the drug supply follow the protocol exactly.

T: How does randomisation work in IRT end to end?
V: randFlow
K: randomisation flow, randomize subject, allocation, kit assignment, randomization end to end
X: Randomisation is the heart of IRT. A study is one clinical trial set up in our system, with treatment arms, like the new drug versus placebo, and randomisation decides, by chance, which arm each patient gets, so the comparison is fair.
X: End to end: a site user selects a screened patient and confirms they're eligible, answering the protocol's eligibility questions and entering stratification factors, like age group. The UI calls the randomisation API with an idempotency key, so a retry after a timeout can't randomise the patient twice. The .NET service checks the user's role at that site, that the subject is in the right state, and that the study and site are open. Then, inside one database transaction with locking, it takes the next available slot from the randomisation list for the patient's stratum, assigns a randomisation number, picks a kit of the right type from the site's stock, writes the audit record, and adds an outbox event.
X: The response gives the site the randomisation number and the kit number. The kit number hides the treatment, so blinded users never see the arm. After that, events trigger the rest: supply checks that may order resupply for the site, and notifications to the study team.
X: The key principle is strong consistency: exactly one correct assignment per patient, and two sites can never get the same slot.

T: What are randomisation lists, block randomisation and stratification?
K: randomisation list, block randomization, stratification, strata, allocation ratio, dynamic randomisation, minimisation
X: These are the rules that decide how patients are assigned to treatment groups in a study, which is one clinical trial set up in IRT.
X: A randomisation list is a pre-generated, ordered list of slots, each with a randomisation number and a treatment arm. It's usually generated by an unblinded statistician, uploaded securely, and never shown to blinded users. When a patient is randomised, IRT gives them the next free slot.
X: Block randomisation keeps the groups balanced over time. The list is made of small blocks, say blocks of four for a one-to-one ratio, where each block contains exactly two of each arm in a random order. So at any point, the groups can't drift far apart. Block sizes are often varied, so site staff can't guess the next assignment.
X: Stratification makes sure important factors are balanced too. If age or region could affect the result, the study defines strata, like "under sixty-five in Europe", and there's a separate list, or a separate sequence of blocks, for each stratum. The site enters the factors at randomisation, and IRT picks from that stratum's list.
X: Some studies use dynamic methods, like minimisation, which compute the assignment at that moment to minimise imbalance, instead of using a fixed list.
X: Technically, the critical part is allocating the next slot safely, in a transaction with locking, so two sites can never take the same one.

T: What is re-randomisation in IRT?
K: re-randomisation, rerandomisation, rerandomization, re-randomization, rerandomize, second randomization, crossover, extension study, treatment switch
X: Re-randomisation means randomising a patient again, later in the same study, to assign them to a new treatment group. A study here is one clinical trial set up in our IRT, and its protocol decides whether re-randomisation exists.
X: It's used in trials with more than one stage. For example, after a first treatment period, patients who respond might be randomised again to either continue the drug or switch to placebo, to see if the benefit lasts. Or in a crossover design, or when patients move into an extension phase. The rules can depend on the first assignment or on the patient's response.
X: In IRT, it works much like the first randomisation, but with extra rules. Only patients in the right state can be re-randomised, for example randomised, still active, and at the right visit. The second assignment uses its own randomisation list or scheme, often stratified by the first arm or by response. The original assignment is never overwritten; the patient's history keeps both, with full audit. Blinding still applies, so site users only see kit numbers. And the kits dispensed from then on follow the new arm.
X: Technically, the same safeguards matter: an idempotency key, a database transaction with locking for the slot, and strict state checks. [A double re-randomisation is exactly the kind of bug the design must make impossible.]

T: What is DARM (Drug Accountability and Return Management)?
V: darmFlow
K: darm, drug accountability, return management, reconciliation, drug destruction, kit returns
X: DARM stands for Drug Accountability and Return Management. In a clinical study, which is one clinical trial set up in IRT, every single drug kit must be accounted for, from the depot to the patient and back. Regulators require it, and sponsors need it to know the drug was used correctly.
X: The lifecycle looks like this. A kit is shipped to a site, and the site confirms it was received in good condition. At a visit, it's dispensed to a patient, which IRT records against the subject. At the next visit, the patient returns the kit, used or partly used, or reports it lost. The site records the return, often with the quantity, like the number of tablets left, which also shows whether the patient took the medication as prescribed, called compliance.
X: Then comes reconciliation: a monitor checks that the counts match, kits dispensed versus returned versus still in stock, including expired or damaged kits at the site. Once reconciled, kits are either destroyed at the site with a record, or sent back to a depot for destruction, and IRT tracks those return shipments too.
X: Technically, DARM is about accurate state transitions for each kit, with rules on which change is allowed when, a full audit trail, and reports that sponsors and monitors rely on. [Our DARM screens are among the newer React modules.]

T: What is blinding, and how does emergency unblinding work in IRT?
K: blinding, unblinding, emergency unblinding, double blind, unblinded users, code break
X: Blinding means hiding which treatment a patient is getting, so it can't influence the results. In a double-blind study, which is a clinical trial where neither the patient nor the site staff know, everyone sees only kit numbers, and the kits look identical.
X: IRT protects blinding in a few ways. The treatment arm is never shown to blinded users or returned by APIs they call. Kit numbers are random, so they don't reveal the arm. Some roles must be unblinded to do their job, like an unblinded pharmacist or the supply team, and their access is separated and audited. Even reports and emails are designed so they don't leak the arm through things like counts by treatment.
X: Emergency unblinding, also called a code break, is for patient safety. If a patient has a serious medical event and the doctor must know what they're taking, an authorised investigator can unblind that one patient in IRT. The system asks for a reason, often requires confirmation, reveals the arm only for that patient, records exactly who did it, when and why in the audit trail, and notifies the sponsor's study team. Depending on the protocol, the patient may then have to stop treatment.
X: Technically, it's role-based access control plus field-level protection, with the unblinding action strongly audited, because protecting blinding is as important as protecting the data itself.

T: How does IRT dispense kits and manage site resupply?
K: kit dispensing, resupply, drug supply, depot, buffer, expiry, shipments, trial supply management
X: In a study, which is one clinical trial set up in IRT, drug supply makes sure every site has the right kits for its patients, without running out and without wasting expensive drug.
X: Dispensing happens at visits. When a patient is randomised, or comes for a dispensing visit, IRT looks at the patient's arm and the visit schedule to know which kit type and how many, then picks specific kits from that site's stock. It skips kits that are expired, or that would expire before the next visit, and kits that are damaged or quarantined. The site gets only kit numbers, so blinding is protected.
X: Resupply runs in the background. Each site has stock levels configured, like a trigger level and a maximum, often per kit type. When stock drops below the trigger, after dispensing or as expiry approaches, IRT creates a shipment order from the assigned depot, so the site is topped up. Initial shipments are sent when a site is activated. Shipments are then tracked through depot and courier systems, which in our case connect through SFTP file integrations and APIs, until the site confirms receipt.
X: Technically, dispensing must be transactional and consistent, while resupply is event-driven: dispensing publishes an event, and supply and shipment services react asynchronously. After that, DARM tracks the kits through return and destruction.
